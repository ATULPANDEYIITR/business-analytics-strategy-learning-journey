# Unit Economics: CAC, LTV, Contribution Margin and Payback

## 1. Domain Overview

Unit economics measures the financial performance of an individual customer, transaction, subscription, or other defined business unit.

For subscription businesses, the customer is commonly used as the analytical unit. The central objective is to determine whether the economic value generated by a customer justifies the expenditure required to acquire and serve that customer.

This implementation examines four connected financial measures:

- Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC)
- Customer Lifetime Value (LTV)
- Contribution Margin
- CAC Payback Period

It also implements cohort analysis, acquisition channel comparisons, sensitivity analysis, policy evaluation, and financial reporting.

The five implementations use a subscription business operating in INR, with acquisition campaigns, customer cohorts, monthly revenue observations, variable costs, and customer lifetime assumptions.

## 2. Core Financial Concepts

### 2.1 Customer Acquisition Cost

Customer Acquisition Cost measures the expenditure required to acquire one new customer.

The implementation includes three acquisition expenditure categories:

- Advertising expenditure
- Sales expenditure
- Marketing expenditure

The calculation is:

\[
CAC =
\frac{Advertising + Sales + Marketing}
{New\ Customers}
\]

Example:

| Cost category | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Advertising | ₹30,000 |
| Sales | ₹10,000 |
| Marketing | ₹10,000 |
| Total acquisition cost | ₹50,000 |
| New customers | 100 |
| CAC | ₹500 |

CAC must be calculated over a clearly defined acquisition period and using a consistent expenditure allocation methodology.

Organic acquisition does not necessarily have zero CAC. Content production, marketing personnel, infrastructure, and other acquisition-related expenses can still contribute to acquisition expenditure.

The Python and JavaScript implementations calculate CAC using acquisition records. The C++ and Java implementations use explicit acquisition domain objects. PostgreSQL stores acquisition expenditure at campaign-period level.

**Important distinction:** Campaign-level CAC is an aggregate measure. Dividing campaign expenditure by acquired customers does not establish the exact individual acquisition cost of every customer.

### 2.2 Contribution Margin

Contribution Margin measures the amount of revenue remaining after deducting variable costs associated with delivering the product or service.

\[
Contribution = Revenue - Variable\ Costs
\]

\[
Contribution\ Margin =
\frac{Revenue - Variable\ Costs}{Revenue}
\times 100
\]

Example:

| Metric | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Monthly revenue | ₹1,000 |
| Variable cost | ₹300 |
| Monthly contribution | ₹700 |
| Contribution margin | 70% |

Variable costs may include:

- Payment processing charges
- Customer-specific hosting expenses
- Transaction fees
- Delivery costs
- Usage-based service expenses
- Customer support costs that vary with usage

A contribution margin of 70% means that ₹700 remains from every ₹1,000 of revenue after the included variable costs.

This amount is available to cover fixed operating expenses and generate operating profit.

Contribution margin is not equivalent to net profit. Fixed salaries, rent, research expenditure, corporate overhead, and other excluded expenses can materially affect actual profitability.

The implementations reject negative revenue and invalid financial inputs. They also distinguish zero revenue with zero costs from a situation involving positive variable costs without revenue.

### 2.3 Customer Lifetime Value

Customer Lifetime Value estimates the economic value generated over the expected customer relationship.

Two different LTV measures are important.

**Revenue-based LTV**

\[
Gross\ LTV =
Monthly\ Revenue \times Expected\ Lifetime
\]

**Contribution-based LTV**

\[
Contribution\ LTV =
Monthly\ Contribution \times Expected\ Lifetime
\]

Example:

| Metric | Value |
|---|---:|
| Monthly revenue | ₹1,000 |
| Monthly variable cost | ₹300 |
| Monthly contribution | ₹700 |
| Expected lifetime | 18 months |
| Revenue-based LTV | ₹18,000 |
| Contribution-based LTV | ₹12,600 |

Revenue-based LTV measures expected revenue rather than the economic value remaining after variable costs.

Contribution-based LTV is therefore more suitable for comparing customer value with acquisition expenditure when the objective is to evaluate contribution economics.

The implementations use expected lifetime as an explicit input. They do not assume that an expected lifetime is automatically an observed customer lifespan.

#### Churn-based lifetime estimation

Monthly churn measures the proportion of customers expected to discontinue during a monthly period.

For a simplified constant-churn model:

\[
Expected\ Lifetime \approx \frac{1}{Monthly\ Churn\ Rate}
\]

For example, a monthly churn rate of 5% produces an approximate expected lifetime of 20 months under the simplified geometric survival assumption.

This approximation depends on stable churn, consistent observation periods, and a suitable customer population.

The implementations preserve churn as an explicit domain attribute while using expected lifetime for the main LTV calculation.

### 2.4 LTV/CAC Ratio

The LTV/CAC ratio compares customer economic value against acquisition expenditure.

\[
LTV/CAC =
\frac{Contribution\ LTV}{CAC}
\]

Example:

\[
LTV/CAC = \frac{12,600}{500} = 25.2
\]

A ratio of 25.2 indicates that estimated contribution LTV is 25.2 times the acquisition cost.

The ratio should be interpreted alongside:

- Contribution margin
- Customer retention
- Payback period
- Fixed operating expenses
- Acquisition channel
- Customer cohort
- Estimation uncertainty

A ratio of 3 is frequently used as an illustrative growth benchmark, but it is not a universal profitability threshold.

The Python, JavaScript, C++, Java, and SQL implementations use contribution-based LTV rather than treating gross revenue as equivalent to customer economic value.

### 2.5 CAC Payback Period

CAC Payback Period measures how long it takes to recover acquisition expenditure through monthly customer contribution.

\[
Payback =
\frac{CAC}{Monthly\ Contribution}
\]

Example:

\[
Payback = \frac{500}{700} = 0.7143\ months
\]

A payback period of approximately 0.71 months means that the expected monthly contribution is sufficient to recover the acquisition cost within the first monthly period under the simplified model.

Payback is not the same as customer lifetime.

- Payback measures the time required to recover acquisition expenditure.
- Lifetime measures the expected duration of the customer relationship.
- LTV measures the estimated value generated throughout that relationship.

When monthly contribution is zero, payback is undefined. The implementations represent this condition explicitly instead of dividing by zero.

## 3. Relationship Between the Four Metrics

The metrics should be evaluated together rather than independently.

| Metric | Financial question | Primary input |
|---|---|---|
| CAC | What does customer acquisition cost? | Acquisition expenditure and new customers |
| Contribution Margin | How much revenue remains after variable costs? | Revenue and variable costs |
| LTV | What economic value does a customer generate? | Contribution and lifetime |
| Payback | How long does acquisition cost recovery take? | CAC and monthly contribution |
| LTV/CAC | Does customer value justify acquisition expenditure? | Contribution LTV and CAC |

The analytical relationship is:

Acquisition Expenditure
→ New Customers
→ CAC

Monthly Revenue
→ Variable Cost Deduction
→ Contribution Margin
→ Monthly Contribution

Monthly Contribution
→ Expected Lifetime
→ Contribution LTV

CAC + Monthly Contribution
→ Payback Period

Contribution LTV + CAC
→ LTV/CAC Ratio

The resulting metrics support acquisition investment, pricing, customer retention, channel allocation, and growth decisions.

## 4. Python Implementation

### 4.1 Domain Modeling

The Python script uses immutable dataclasses for financial inputs and results.

`Acquisition` represents campaign-level acquisition expenditure.

`CustomerEconomics` represents customer-level financial assumptions.

`UnitEconomicsResult` stores calculated financial indicators.

`Channel` uses an enumeration to prevent inconsistent channel labels.

The use of frozen dataclasses prevents accidental modification of financial input records after validation.

### 4.2 Calculation Engine

`UnitEconomicsEngine` separates the main financial calculations into independent methods.

| Method | Responsibility |
|---|---|
| `calculate_cac()` | Calculates acquisition cost per customer |
| `calculate_contribution_margin()` | Calculates contribution and margin |
| `calculate_ltv()` | Calculates gross and contribution LTV |
| `calculate_payback()` | Calculates CAC recovery time |
| `evaluate()` | Combines the metrics into a customer assessment |

The engine checks for invalid denominators, negative costs, mismatched channels, non-finite values, and invalid lifetime assumptions.

### 4.3 Cohort Analysis

`CohortAnalyzer` groups customers by cohort and calculates aggregate revenue, variable cost, contribution, and contribution margin.

This prevents customer-level observations from being confused with aggregate cohort performance.

### 4.4 Channel Analysis

`ChannelAnalyzer` aggregates expenditure and customer acquisition volumes by channel.

CAC is calculated using total expenditure divided by total acquired customers.

This weighted approach is preferable to taking an unweighted average of individual campaign CAC values.

### 4.5 Sensitivity Analysis

`SensitivityAnalyzer` evaluates changes in monthly revenue while holding variable cost, lifetime, and CAC constant.

The scenarios include:

- 20% revenue reduction
- 10% revenue reduction
- Baseline revenue
- 10% revenue increase
- 20% revenue increase

The results show how revenue assumptions influence contribution LTV and the LTV/CAC ratio.

### 4.6 Reporting and Validation

The script includes:

- JSON report generation
- Atomic temporary-file replacement
- Financial validation
- Assertion-based tests
- Structured logging
- Exception handling

The generated report separates customer analysis, channel analysis, cohort analysis, and sensitivity results.

Run using Python 3.10 or later:

`python unit_economics.py`

The output is saved as `unit_economics_report.json` in the working directory.

## 5. JavaScript Implementation

### 5.1 Event-Driven Financial Processing

The JavaScript implementation uses Node.js and its built-in `EventEmitter` class.

`AnalyticsService` processes financial records and emits events when calculations are completed.

Two events are implemented:

- `calculated`: Indicates that a customer economics evaluation has completed.
- `policyViolation`: Indicates that an evaluation does not satisfy the configured financial policy.

This architecture separates financial calculation from downstream reporting and monitoring.

### 5.2 Immutable Financial Objects

`AcquisitionRecord` represents acquisition expenditure.

`Subscription` represents customer-level subscription economics.

Both objects validate their inputs during construction and are frozen after initialization.

This prevents accidental modification of the financial records during processing.

### 5.3 Cohort Registry

`CohortRegistry` uses nested JavaScript Maps.

The outer Map stores cohort identifiers, while the inner Map stores customer records.

This supports efficient customer lookup and duplicate detection.

A customer cannot be inserted twice into the same cohort.

### 5.4 Channel Performance

`ChannelPerformance` aggregates acquisition expenditure using a Map.

The implementation calculates channel-level CAC using aggregate expenditure and aggregate customer counts.

Channels are sorted by CAC to support acquisition cost comparisons.

### 5.5 Financial Policy Evaluation

`EconomicsPolicy` provides configurable business thresholds.

The default policy contains:

| Policy | Threshold |
|---|---:|
| Minimum LTV/CAC | 3 |
| Maximum payback | 12 months |
| Minimum contribution margin | 50% |

A financial evaluation can fail one or several policy conditions.

The policy engine returns a structured list of violations rather than terminating the entire processing operation.

These thresholds are configurable business assumptions, not universal financial standards.

### 5.6 Asynchronous Reporting

The report exporter uses Node.js filesystem promises.

It writes to a temporary file and renames the file after successful completion.

The temporary filename uses a UUID to reduce collision risk.

Run using:

`node unit_economics.js`

The implementation requires no third-party npm packages.

## 6. C++17 Implementation

### 6.1 Financial Domain Structures

The C++ program uses structs and enumerations to model acquisition, customer economics, calculated results, reviews, status checks, and governance conditions.

`UnitEconomicsEngine` is responsible for validating inputs and calculating the financial metrics.

`Economics` stores the calculation results, including an optional payback value.

`std::optional` is used because payback cannot be calculated when monthly contribution is zero.

### 6.2 STL-Based Cohort Aggregation

`CohortAnalyzer` uses `std::map` to group customer records by cohort.

Each cohort stores:

- Customer count
- Aggregate revenue
- Aggregate variable cost

The implementation calculates contribution margin after aggregation.

Using a map provides ordered cohort reporting and logarithmic lookup and insertion complexity.

### 6.3 Repository Governance Integration

The program also models an investment-related Pull Request evaluation workflow.

This is a governance demonstration showing how financial policy checks can become automated conditions in a controlled development workflow.

`PullRequest` stores proposed branch changes, review decisions, status checks, and calculated economics.

`BranchProtection` defines repository-level restrictions.

`GovernanceEngine` evaluates:

- Pull Request state
- Source and target branches
- Merge conflicts
- Review eligibility
- Stale approvals
- Requested changes
- Required status checks
- Resolved conversations
- Linear history
- Contribution positivity
- LTV/CAC requirements
- Payback requirements

The financial engine calculates unit economics. The governance engine independently evaluates whether the associated financial and repository conditions permit merging.

This separation avoids treating a financial calculation as equivalent to repository-level enforcement.

### 6.4 Complexity

For \(n\) customers:

| Operation | Complexity |
|---|---|
| Individual financial calculation | O(1) |
| Cohort aggregation | O(n log k) |
| Cohort lookup in ordered map | O(log k) |
| Review aggregation | O(r log r) |
| Governance evaluation | O(r log r) |

Here, \(k\) represents the number of cohorts and \(r\) represents the number of reviews.

Run using a C++17 compiler:

`g++ -std=c++17 -O2 -Wall -Wextra -pedantic unit_economics.cpp -o unit_economics`

Execute:

`./unit_economics`

## 7. Java 17 Enterprise Implementation

### 7.1 Enterprise Domain Architecture

The Java implementation uses records, enums, interfaces, collections, and service classes.

The main financial types are:

- `Acquisition`
- `Customer`
- `UnitEconomics`
- `BranchProtectionPolicy`
- `EligibilityResult`

The `EconomicsService` interface separates the calculation contract from its implementation.

`DefaultEconomicsService` provides the standard calculation implementation.

This design allows alternative calculation strategies to be introduced without changing the calling code.

### 7.2 Validation and Financial Precision

The `Validation` class centralizes input validation.

The calculation service uses `BigDecimal` for rounding financial results to two decimal places.

This is preferable to using uncontrolled floating-point rounding for displayed financial amounts.

The underlying calculations still use Java primitive floating-point values, so a production accounting implementation requiring strict decimal arithmetic should use `BigDecimal` throughout the calculation pipeline.

### 7.3 Pull Request State Management

`PullRequest` implements explicit state transitions.

| Current state | Operation | Result |
|---|---|---|
| DRAFT | Open | OPEN |
| OPEN | Close | CLOSED |
| CLOSED | Reopen | OPEN |
| OPEN | Merge | MERGED |

Invalid state transitions generate domain exceptions.

The implementation also models base-branch synchronization.

When the base commit changes, earlier review decisions become stale. Stale approvals cannot satisfy the approval requirement.

### 7.4 Approval Eligibility

Approval decisions are evaluated using reviewer identity and eligibility.

An approval counts only when:

- The reviewer is eligible.
- The approval has not become stale.
- The review state is APPROVED.
- The reviewer has not been counted already.

A requested-changes decision from an eligible, non-stale reviewer prevents the proposed merge.

This is a simplified enterprise review policy. Actual repository platforms may have additional dismissal, permission, ownership, and bypass rules.

### 7.5 Merge Eligibility Service

`MergeEligibilityService` evaluates repository and financial conditions.

It checks:

- Pull Request state
- Merge conflicts
- Direct-push restrictions
- Force-push restrictions
- Linear-history requirements
- Required status checks
- Unresolved conversations
- Eligible approvals
- Contribution margin-related financial results
- LTV/CAC threshold
- Payback threshold

The service produces an immutable `EligibilityResult`.

A merge is permitted only when the violation list is empty.

### 7.6 Cohort Analytics

`CohortAnalytics` uses Java Maps and collection operations to aggregate customers.

The resulting cohort metrics are exposed as unmodifiable maps.

This provides a simple immutable reporting interface.

Compile and execute:

`javac --release 17 UnitEconomicsEnterprise.java`

`java UnitEconomicsEnterprise`

No external Java libraries are required.

## 8. PostgreSQL Relational Architecture

### 8.1 Database Structure

The PostgreSQL implementation uses the `unit_economics` schema.

The relational structure separates acquisition, customer, financial, assumption, and evaluation data.

| Table | Purpose |
|---|---|
| `organizations` | Business entities and currency configuration |
| `acquisition_channels` | Controlled acquisition channel definitions |
| `campaigns` | Acquisition campaign records |
| `acquisition_periods` | Campaign expenditure and acquired customer counts |
| `customer_cohorts` | Customer cohort definitions |
| `customers` | Customer acquisition and lifecycle records |
| `customer_monthly_economics` | Monthly revenue and variable cost observations |
| `customer_lifetime_assumptions` | Lifetime and churn assumptions |
| `economics_policies` | Configurable financial decision thresholds |
| `unit_economics_evaluations` | Stored financial evaluation results |
| `economics_audit_log` | Changes to monthly financial observations |

### 8.2 Relational Relationships

The main entity relationships are:

Organization
→ Acquisition Channels
→ Campaigns
→ Acquisition Periods
→ Customers

Organization
→ Customer Cohorts
→ Customers

Customers
→ Monthly Economics

Organization
→ Lifetime Assumptions

Organization
→ Economics Policies

Customers
→ Unit Economics Evaluations

Monthly Economics
→ Audit Log

Foreign keys maintain referential integrity between related entities.

Unique constraints prevent duplicate organization names, campaign names within an organization, customer references, cohort names, and monthly customer observations.

### 8.3 Generated Columns

The monthly economics table uses PostgreSQL generated columns.

`net_revenue` is derived from revenue, refunds, and service credits.

`contribution` is derived from revenue after refunds and service credits, less variable cost.

These values are computed by the database rather than independently supplied by applications.

This reduces the possibility of inconsistent stored financial calculations.

### 8.4 Financial Functions

Three independent SQL functions provide core calculations.

| Function | Purpose |
|---|---|
| `calculate_cac()` | Aggregates campaign expenditure and customer acquisition |
| `calculate_contribution_margin()` | Calculates contribution margin |
| `calculate_payback()` | Calculates CAC recovery time |

The `evaluate_unit_economics()` function combines customer observations, acquisition expenditure, lifetime assumptions, and organizational policies.

### 8.5 Evaluation Procedure

`process_customer_economics()` performs the financial evaluation and stores the result.

It uses an upsert operation to avoid inserting a duplicate evaluation for the same customer, acquisition period, and evaluation date.

The procedure records:

- CAC
- Monthly contribution
- Contribution margin
- Gross LTV
- Contribution LTV
- LTV/CAC ratio
- Payback period
- Evaluation status

The operation executes within the calling transaction.

A failed evaluation raises an exception rather than storing an incomplete result.

### 8.6 Reporting Views

Three reporting views support different analytical requirements.

`cohort_economics` provides cohort-level customer counts, revenue, variable cost, contribution, and contribution margin.

`channel_acquisition_performance` compares campaign acquisition expenditure and CAC by channel.

`customer_economics_report` combines customer-level acquisition expenditure, observed financial performance, and lifetime assumptions.

The distinction between customer-level and campaign-level CAC remains important when interpreting these views.

### 8.7 Audit Mechanism

The `audit_economics_change()` trigger function records inserts, updates, and deletions affecting monthly customer economics.

The audit table records:

- Table name
- Operation type
- Affected record identifier
- Timestamp
- Database user

This provides an operational record of financial observation changes.

The audit mechanism is not a complete immutable audit architecture. Production environments should restrict audit table permissions and implement additional controls against unauthorized modification.

### 8.8 Indexing Strategy

The database includes indexes on:

- Campaign and acquisition period
- Customer cohort
- Customer acquisition period
- Customer status
- Reporting month
- Customer and reporting month
- Evaluation status and date
- LTV/CAC ratio
- Active organizational policies

These indexes support common filtering, joining, and reporting operations.

Indexes improve retrieval performance but increase storage requirements and write overhead.

Query plans should be evaluated using `EXPLAIN (ANALYZE, BUFFERS)` with representative production data.

## 9. Cohort Economics

Cohort analysis groups customers according to a shared acquisition period or another meaningful classification.

For example, customers acquired during the first quarter of 2026 belong to the `2026-Q1` cohort.

The cohort can be examined over subsequent reporting periods.

A cohort analysis can identify:

- Changes in monthly contribution
- Deteriorating retention
- Differences in acquisition channel quality
- Changes in customer purchasing behavior
- Variations in contribution margin
- Differences in expected customer lifetime

A critical distinction exists between aggregate cohort economics and individual customer economics.

An aggregate cohort contribution margin is calculated using total contribution divided by total revenue.

It should not be calculated by taking an unweighted average of customer margin percentages.

The Python, JavaScript, C++, Java, and SQL implementations use aggregation-based approaches.

## 10. Acquisition Channel Analysis

Different acquisition channels may produce substantially different CAC and customer economics.

Consider the following illustrative acquisition data:

| Channel | Total acquisition expenditure | Customers | CAC |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Paid Search | ₹50,000 | 100 | ₹500 |
| Social | ₹30,000 | 120 | ₹250 |
| Referral | ₹10,000 | 100 | ₹100 |
| Organic | ₹10,000 | 100 | ₹100 |

A low CAC does not automatically indicate a superior channel.

A channel may acquire customers at a lower cost but generate:

- Lower monthly revenue
- Higher variable costs
- Higher churn
- Shorter customer lifetime
- Lower contribution LTV

The correct evaluation compares acquisition expenditure with the economic value of the customers acquired.

Channel-level decisions should therefore consider CAC, contribution margin, LTV/CAC, payback, and retention together.

## 11. Sensitivity and Scenario Analysis

Financial projections depend on assumptions.

Small changes in revenue, variable costs, customer lifetime, or churn can materially affect projected LTV.

Sensitivity analysis evaluates the impact of changes in one or more assumptions.

For example, the implementations examine the following revenue scenarios:

| Scenario | Revenue change |
|---|---:|
| Adverse | -20% |
| Moderate decline | -10% |
| Baseline | 0% |
| Moderate growth | +10% |
| Strong growth | +20% |

When variable costs remain constant, a revenue reduction directly reduces monthly contribution.

The contribution LTV and LTV/CAC ratio consequently decline.

This is a simplified one-variable sensitivity analysis. More comprehensive financial models should evaluate simultaneous changes in:

- Customer acquisition expenditure
- Customer conversion rates
- Monthly churn
- Revenue per customer
- Variable cost per customer
- Lifetime assumptions
- Refunds and service credits

Scenario analysis should also distinguish changes in assumptions from changes supported by observed customer data.

## 12. Financial Decision Rules

The implementations use illustrative policy thresholds to classify customer economics.

| Condition | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| Monthly contribution is zero or negative | Customer economics are structurally unfavorable |
| Contribution LTV is less than or equal to CAC | Acquisition cost is not recovered over expected lifetime |
| LTV/CAC is below policy | Customer value does not satisfy the selected threshold |
| Payback exceeds policy | Capital recovery is slower than the permitted period |
| Contribution margin is below policy | Variable cost structure requires investigation |
| All configured requirements pass | Financial evaluation satisfies the selected policy |

These classifications must not be confused with audited accounting profit.

A positive contribution LTV does not establish that a company is profitable after fixed operating expenses, taxes, financing costs, and other obligations.

## 13. Edge Cases and Failure Conditions

The implementations explicitly handle several financial edge cases.

| Condition | Expected behavior |
|---|---|
| Zero new customers | Reject CAC calculation |
| Negative acquisition expenditure | Reject input |
| Negative revenue | Reject input |
| Negative variable cost | Reject input |
| Zero revenue and zero variable cost | Zero contribution margin |
| Zero revenue and positive variable cost | Reject inconsistent observation |
| Zero monthly contribution | Payback is undefined |
| Negative monthly contribution | Negative contribution classification |
| Zero CAC and positive contribution LTV | Ratio is mathematically unbounded |
| Invalid churn rate | Reject assumption |
| Non-positive expected lifetime | Reject assumption |
| Acquisition channel mismatch | Reject evaluation |
| Missing financial observations | Reject or report insufficient data |

The treatment of zero CAC requires particular care. A zero acquisition cost may occur in genuinely organic acquisition, but it may also indicate that some acquisition expenditure has not been allocated.

A mathematically unbounded ratio must not be interpreted automatically as unlimited economic value.

## 15. Security and Financial Data Integrity

Unit economics systems process commercially sensitive information.

Relevant security controls include:

- Role-based access to customer financial records
- Restricted permissions on acquisition expenditure
- Controlled access to policy configuration
- Validation of all financial inputs
- Secure handling of exported reports
- Audit logging of financial changes
- Encryption in transit and at rest
- Separation of reporting and administrative permissions
- Protection of customer identifiers
- Controlled retention of historical observations

The PostgreSQL implementation provides foreign keys, checks, unique constraints, generated columns, and an audit trigger.

These mechanisms enforce selected integrity conditions within the database.

They do not replace application authorization, infrastructure security, backup policies, or independent audit controls.

## 16. Important Modeling Limitations

### 16.1 Simplified Lifetime Assumptions

The implementations use an explicit expected lifetime assumption.

Actual customer lifetime is uncertain and should ideally be estimated using observed retention and survival data.

### 16.2 Constant Monthly Economics

The basic LTV calculation assumes constant monthly contribution.

Real customers may change their usage, subscription tier, transaction volume, and cost profile over time.

### 16.3 Acquisition Cost Allocation

Campaign-level acquisition expenditure is allocated across acquired customers using a simple denominator.

Multi-touch attribution, sales-cycle timing, shared personnel expenditure, and cross-channel attribution require additional allocation rules.

### 16.4 No Discounting of Future Cash Flows

The basic LTV formula does not discount future contribution.

For long customer lifetimes, a discounted cash-flow model may provide a more economically meaningful estimate.

A discounted contribution model can be expressed as:

\[
LTV =
\sum_{t=1}^{T}
\frac{Expected\ Contribution_t \times Survival_t}
{(1+d)^t}
\]

Where:

- \(T\) is the projection horizon.
- \(Expected\ Contribution_t\) is the contribution expected in period \(t\).
- \(Survival_t\) is the probability that the customer remains active.
- \(d\) is the discount rate per period.

### 16.5 Currency and Tax Treatment

The demonstrations use INR and do not implement multi-currency consolidation or a complete tax accounting system.

A production system should establish explicit currency, exchange-rate, tax, refund, and revenue-recognition policies.

### 16.6 Evaluation and Historical Reproducibility

Financial results depend on the data and assumptions available at evaluation time.

Production systems should preserve the versions of the input observations, lifetime assumptions, and financial policies used for each evaluation.

This makes historical comparisons reproducible when assumptions or business rules change.
