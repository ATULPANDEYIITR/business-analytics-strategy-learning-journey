# Business Functions

## Overview

Business functions are the major areas of activity through which an organization creates value, generates revenue, serves customers, manages resources and achieves its strategic objectives.

The major business functions studied are:

* Finance
* Marketing
* Sales
* Operations
* Human Resources (HR)
* Supply Chain

Although these functions have different responsibilities, they are strongly interconnected. A Data Analyst needs to understand both individual functions and the relationships between them because business decisions frequently involve multiple departments.

## Understanding business functions

A business function represents a specific category of business activity.

Finance manages money, financial resources, profitability, risk and financial reporting.

Marketing creates awareness, generates demand, understands customers and builds the company's brand.

Sales converts prospects into customers and generates revenue.

Operations transforms resources into products or services and manages execution, efficiency and quality.

Human Resources manages employees, workforce planning, recruitment, development, performance and retention.

Supply Chain manages sourcing, procurement, inventory, logistics and the movement of materials and products.

## Finance

Finance focuses on the financial health and economic performance of an organization.

Important financial concepts practiced include:

* Revenue
* Cost of Goods Sold
* Gross Profit
* Gross Margin
* Operating Expenses
* Operating Profit
* Operating Margin
* Net Profit
* Net Profit Margin
* EBITDA
* Cash Flow
* Accounts Receivable
* Accounts Payable
* Break-even Analysis
* Return on Investment (ROI)
* Net Present Value (NPV)

### Important Finance formulas

Gross Profit:

`Gross Profit = Revenue - Cost of Goods Sold`

Gross Margin:

`Gross Margin = Gross Profit / Revenue`

Operating Profit:

`Operating Profit = Gross Profit - Operating Expenses`

Net Profit:

`Net Profit = Revenue - Total Applicable Expenses`

Net Profit Margin:

`Net Profit Margin = Net Profit / Revenue`

ROI:

`ROI = (Gain from Investment - Cost of Investment) / Cost of Investment`

Break-even units:

`Break-even Units = Fixed Costs / (Selling Price per Unit - Variable Cost per Unit)`

NPV evaluates future cash flows while considering the time value of money.

## Marketing

Marketing focuses on understanding customers, generating demand and communicating the value of products or services.

The marketing funnel was studied as:

`Awareness -> Interest -> Consideration -> Conversion -> Retention -> Advocacy`

Important marketing concepts and KPIs include:

* Impressions
* Reach
* Clicks
* Click-Through Rate (CTR)
* Conversion Rate
* Cost Per Click (CPC)
* Cost Per Mille (CPM)
* Cost Per Lead (CPL)
* Customer Acquisition Cost (CAC)
* Return on Advertising Spend (ROAS)
* Customer Lifetime Value (LTV)
* Customer Segmentation
* Marketing Funnel Analysis

Important formulas include:

`CTR = Clicks / Impressions`

`CPC = Marketing Cost / Clicks`

`CAC = Marketing Cost / Customers Acquired`

`ROAS = Revenue from Advertising / Advertising Cost`

Customer Lifetime Value can be estimated using purchase value, purchase frequency, customer lifetime and gross margin.

## Sales

Sales converts prospects and opportunities into customers and revenue.

A typical sales process is:

`Lead -> Qualification -> Opportunity -> Proposal -> Negotiation -> Closed Won / Closed Lost`

Sales analysis includes:

* Lead volume
* Qualified leads
* Opportunities
* Proposals
* Closed deals
* Conversion rate
* Average deal size
* Sales pipeline
* Win rate
* Sales cycle
* Quota attainment
* Territory performance
* Sales representative performance
* Forecast accuracy
* Weighted pipeline forecasting

Weighted sales forecasting uses the value of an opportunity multiplied by its estimated probability of closing.

`Expected Revenue = Opportunity Value × Probability of Closing`

Typical inputs include:

* People

Important operations concepts include:

* Productivity

`Labor Productivity = Units Produced / Labor Hours`

`Defect Rate = Defective Units / Total Units`

`Throughput = Output / Processing Time`

`Cycle Time = Total Processing Time / Units Completed`

Major HR activities include:

Important Supply Chain activities include:

* Warehousing

`Stockout Rate = Stockout Orders / Total Orders`

`Supplier On-Time Rate = On-Time Orders / Total Orders`

## Cross-functional business analysis

The six business functions should not be treated as completely independent.

For example:

`Marketing -> Leads -> Sales -> Orders -> Operations -> Supply Chain -> HR -> Finance`

If Marketing increases advertising, the number of leads may increase.

More leads may create more sales opportunities.

More successful sales create more customer orders.

More orders increase the workload for Operations.

Operations may require additional inventory.

Supply Chain may need to increase procurement.

Additional workload may require more employees.

Finance must determine whether the additional revenue creates sufficient additional profit.

This demonstrates why a Data Analyst needs cross-functional business knowledge.

Business analytics therefore converts:

`Business Problem -> Analytical Questions -> Data Requirements -> Analysis -> Insight -> Decision`

## Business performance comparison

Business analysts frequently compare:

* Actual vs Budget
* Actual vs Forecast
* Current Period vs Previous Period
* Current Year vs Previous Year
* Product A vs Product B
* Region A vs Region B
* Customer Segment A vs Customer Segment B

Variance analysis can identify whether actual performance is above or below the expected level.

Variance:

`Variance = Actual - Budget`

Variance percentage:

`Variance % = (Actual - Budget) / Budget`

Year-over-year growth:

`YoY Growth = (Current Year Value - Previous Year Value) / Previous Year Value`

Month-over-month growth:

`MoM Growth = (Current Month Value - Previous Month Value) / Previous Month Value`

## Unit economics

Unit economics evaluates the economics of an individual customer, transaction, product or unit of business.

Important concepts include:

* Customer Acquisition Cost
* Customer Lifetime Value
* Contribution Margin
* Payback Period
* LTV/CAC Ratio

Contribution margin can be calculated as:

`Contribution Margin = Revenue - Variable Costs`

CAC payback period can be estimated as:

`Payback Period = CAC / Monthly Contribution`

The LTV/CAC ratio compares the economic value generated by a customer with the cost of acquiring that customer.

## Process analysis

Business processes can be understood using:

`Input -> Activity -> Output -> Outcome`

For example:

`Marketing Budget -> Advertising -> Leads -> Customers -> Revenue`

Another example is:

`Raw Materials -> Manufacturing -> Finished Goods -> Shipment -> Customer Delivery`

Process analysis helps identify inefficiencies, bottlenecks, delays, quality problems and opportunities for improvement.

## Bottleneck analysis

A bottleneck is the stage in a process that limits the overall capacity of the system.

For example:

* Stage A: 100 units/hour
* Stage B: 40 units/hour
* Stage C: 80 units/hour

Stage B is the bottleneck because it has the lowest capacity.

Improving non-bottleneck stages may not increase total system output if the bottleneck remains unchanged.

## Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analysis examines how a business outcome changes when one or more inputs change.

Examples include:

* What happens to profit if price increases?
* What happens to revenue if conversion falls?
* What happens to profit if material costs increase?
* What happens to demand if marketing expenditure changes?

Sensitivity analysis helps identify variables that have the greatest influence on business outcomes.

## Business analytics maturity

Business analytics can be viewed through four major levels.

### Descriptive analytics

Question:

**What happened?**

Examples:

* Revenue was ₹10 crore.
* Sales declined by 5%.

### Diagnostic analytics

Question:

**Why did it happen?**

Example:

Sales declined because conversion rate decreased.

### Predictive analytics

Question:

**What is likely to happen?**

Example:

Next month's sales are forecast to reach a specific level.

### Prescriptive analytics

Question:

**What should we do?**

Example:

Increase inventory for products expected to experience high demand.

The progression is:

`Descriptive -> Diagnostic -> Predictive -> Prescriptive`

## Causal thinking

Correlation does not automatically prove causation.

For example, if marketing expenditure increases while sales also increase, this does not necessarily mean that all additional sales were caused by marketing.

Other factors may include:

* Seasonality
* Pricing
* Competitor behavior
* Product launches
* Economic conditions
* Distribution expansion
* Sales team changes

A strong analyst therefore asks:

* What changed?
* When did it change?
* What else changed?
* What would have happened without the change?
* Can a counterfactual be established?
* Is there evidence of a causal relationship?

## Cross-functional root-cause analysis

When a business outcome changes, the analyst should investigate the complete chain of drivers.

For example:

`Profit Decline -> Revenue Decline -> Conversion Decline -> Inventory Shortages -> Supplier Delays`

This approach is stronger than simply reporting that profit declined.

The objective is to identify the underlying drivers and determine which business actions can change the outcome.

## Local optimization vs global optimization

Optimizing one department does not necessarily optimize the entire business.

For example:

Marketing may want maximum leads.

Sales may want maximum qualified opportunities.

Operations may want stable demand.

Finance may want profitable growth.

Supply Chain may want predictable demand and manageable inventory.

HR may want sustainable workforce requirements.

These objectives can conflict.

Therefore, business analysis should focus on the overall organizational objective rather than optimizing one function in isolation.

## KPI trees

A KPI tree decomposes a high-level business outcome into its underlying drivers.

For example:

`Profit`

can be decomposed into:

`Profit = Revenue - Cost`

Revenue can then be decomposed into:

`Revenue = Customers × Average Revenue per Customer`

Customers can be decomposed into acquisition and retention.

Costs can be decomposed into:

* Labor
* Materials
* Marketing
* Logistics
* Technology
* Overheads

KPI trees help analysts move from high-level outcomes to the drivers that influence those outcomes.

## Cash Conversion Cycle

The Cash Conversion Cycle measures the amount of time cash is tied up in the operating cycle.

A simplified formula is:

`CCC = Days Inventory Outstanding + Days Sales Outstanding - Days Payable Outstanding`

A lower cash conversion cycle can indicate that a business converts its investment in inventory and receivables into cash more quickly, subject to the company's operating model and financial strategy.

## Cohort analysis

A cohort is a group of customers or entities sharing a common characteristic.

Examples include:

* Customers acquired in January
* Customers acquired during Q1
* Customers who purchased a particular product
* Customers from a particular region

Cohort analysis can be used to study:

* Retention
* Churn
* Revenue
* Customer behavior
* Product performance

Cohort analysis can reveal whether newer customer groups behave differently from older customer groups.

## Business trade-offs

Business decisions frequently involve trade-offs.

For example:

Higher inventory:

`Higher Availability -> Higher Inventory Carrying Cost`

Lower inventory:

`Lower Carrying Cost -> Higher Stockout Risk`

More employees:

`Higher Capacity -> Higher Labor Cost`

Fewer employees:

`Lower Cost -> Potential Capacity or Service Problems`

The role of analytics is to quantify these trade-offs and help management choose an appropriate balance.

## Decision analytics

The ultimate purpose of business analytics is not simply to produce numbers.

The objective is to improve decisions.

A useful business analytics cycle is:

`Data -> Information -> Insight -> Decision -> Action -> Outcome -> Measurement -> Learning`

For example:

A Data Analyst may discover that conversion rate has declined.

The analyst investigates the underlying cause and discovers a mobile checkout problem.

Management decides to fix the checkout process.

The change is implemented.

Conversion improves.

The analyst measures the result and determines whether the intervention generated the expected business impact.

This creates a continuous analytical learning cycle.
