# Revenue & Cost: Revenue Streams, Fixed Costs, Variable Costs and Margins

## Introduction

Revenue and cost analysis is a fundamental part of business management, financial analysis, product management, entrepreneurship, operations, pricing, and strategic decision-making. A business can generate substantial revenue and still make a loss if its costs exceed the economic value generated by its sales.

The central purpose of revenue and cost analysis is to understand how a business converts customer demand into financial outcomes.

The core relationship is:

    Profit = Revenue - Total Cost

This relationship appears simple, but meaningful analysis requires distinguishing between different types of revenue, different categories of costs, and different measures of profitability.

The Python script accompanying this document models these concepts progressively, beginning with basic revenue calculations and extending to contribution margin analysis, break-even analysis, pricing, product mix, operating leverage, sensitivity analysis, capacity constraints, customer economics, and integrated business simulations.

---

# Revenue

Revenue is the monetary value earned by a business from selling products, services, access, subscriptions, licenses, or other offerings.

For a single product:

    Revenue = Selling Price per Unit × Units Sold

For example, if a business sells 1,000 units at 500 currency units per unit:

    Revenue = 500 × 1,000
    Revenue = 500,000

Revenue represents the amount generated from sales before deducting costs.

Revenue should not be confused with profit.

A company with revenue of 10 million may be less profitable than a company with revenue of 5 million if the larger company has substantially higher costs.

The script demonstrates revenue calculation through the `calculate_revenue()` function.

---

# Revenue Streams

A revenue stream is a distinct source through which a business earns money.

A business may have one or several revenue streams.

Examples include:

- Product sales
- Subscription fees
- Consulting services
- Advertising
- Licensing
- Commissions
- Transaction fees
- Maintenance contracts
- Membership fees
- Professional services

A multi-stream business can calculate total revenue as:

    Total Revenue = Revenue Stream 1
                  + Revenue Stream 2
                  + Revenue Stream 3
                  + ...

The script represents each stream with the `RevenueStream` data class.

Each revenue stream has:

- A name
- A unit price
- A quantity or number of units sold
- A calculated revenue value

This structure is useful because businesses frequently need to understand not only how much revenue they earn, but also where that revenue originates.

Revenue concentration can become a business risk when a large percentage of revenue depends on a single product, customer category, or revenue stream.

# Cost

A cost is a resource consumed by a business in order to operate, produce goods, deliver services, acquire customers, or maintain its infrastructure.

Costs can be classified in several ways.

The script focuses primarily on:

- Fixed costs
- Variable costs
- Mixed costs
- Step-fixed costs

The classification depends on how a cost behaves when business activity changes.

---

# Fixed Costs

Fixed costs are costs that generally remain unchanged when production or sales volume changes within a relevant operating range and time period.

Examples include:

- Office rent
- Salaried administrative employees
- Insurance
- Software subscriptions
- Certain technology infrastructure costs
- Depreciation
- Lease payments

A simple model represents fixed cost as:

    Total Fixed Cost = Constant for the relevant range

For example:

    Units Sold: 100
    Fixed Cost: 100,000

and:

    Units Sold: 500

may both have the same fixed cost of:

    100,000

This does not mean fixed costs remain unchanged forever.

A business may need additional employees, office space, equipment, or infrastructure after crossing a certain level of activity.

The script demonstrates this distinction through both ordinary fixed costs and step-fixed costs.

---

# Variable Costs

Variable costs change with business activity.

For example, if raw material costs 150 per unit:

    100 units require:
    150 × 100 = 15,000

    1,000 units require:
    150 × 1,000 = 150,000

The basic formula is:

    Total Variable Cost
    = Variable Cost per Unit × Units

Examples of variable costs include:

- Raw materials
- Packaging
- Sales commissions
- Payment processing charges
- Per-unit shipping
- Transaction fees
- Usage-based cloud infrastructure
- Contractor payments linked directly to projects

The script represents variable costs using the `VariableCost` data class.

A business can have several variable cost components associated with one product.

For example:

    Raw Materials: 150
    Packaging: 20
    Payment Processing: 10

Total variable cost per unit:

    150 + 20 + 10 = 180

---

# Total Cost

Total cost combines fixed and variable costs.

The standard relationship is:

    Total Cost
    = Fixed Cost + Total Variable Cost

For a single product:

    Total Cost
    = Fixed Cost
    + Variable Cost per Unit × Units

This distinction is important because fixed costs and variable costs behave differently as sales volume changes.

When sales increase:

- Total variable costs generally increase.
- Fixed costs may remain unchanged within the relevant operating range.
- Fixed cost per unit decreases because the same fixed cost is distributed across more units.

---

# Profit

Profit measures the financial amount remaining after deducting the relevant costs from revenue.

The simplest relationship is:

    Profit = Revenue - Total Cost

If revenue is greater than total cost, the business generates a profit.

If total cost exceeds revenue, the business generates a loss.

If revenue equals total cost, the business is at break-even.

Profit can be measured at several stages of a financial statement, resulting in different profit measures and margins.

---

# Contribution Margin

Contribution margin is one of the most important concepts in cost-volume-profit analysis.

It measures how much revenue remains after variable costs are deducted.

For a single unit:

    Contribution Margin per Unit
    = Selling Price per Unit
    - Variable Cost per Unit

For total sales:

    Total Contribution Margin
    = Total Revenue
    - Total Variable Costs

Contribution margin is used to:

1. Cover fixed costs.
2. Generate profit after fixed costs have been covered.

For example:

    Selling Price = 1,000
    Variable Cost = 600

Contribution margin per unit:

    1,000 - 600 = 400

Every unit sold contributes 400 toward fixed costs and, after fixed costs are covered, toward profit.

---

# Contribution Margin Ratio

Contribution margin ratio expresses contribution margin as a percentage of revenue.

The formula is:

    Contribution Margin Ratio
    = Contribution Margin / Revenue

For one unit:

    Contribution Margin Ratio
    = (Price - Variable Cost) / Price

Using:

    Price = 1,000
    Variable Cost = 600

The contribution margin ratio is:

    (1,000 - 600) / 1,000
    = 40%

This means that 40% of revenue remains available to cover fixed costs and profit after variable costs are deducted.

The script calculates this ratio using the `contribution_margin_ratio()` function.

---

# Positive, Zero and Negative Contribution Margins

A positive contribution margin means:

    Selling Price > Variable Cost

Each additional sale contributes toward covering fixed costs.

A zero contribution margin means:

    Selling Price = Variable Cost

Additional sales generate no amount toward fixed costs.

A negative contribution margin means:

    Selling Price < Variable Cost

Each additional sale increases the loss before considering fixed costs.

This is an important edge case.

A business with negative contribution margins cannot solve its fundamental economics simply by increasing sales volume. More sales increase the total negative contribution unless pricing or variable costs change.

---

# Break-Even Analysis

Break-even analysis determines the sales level at which total revenue equals total cost.

At break-even:

    Profit = 0

The break-even formula in units is:

    Break-even Units
    = Fixed Cost / Contribution Margin per Unit

For example:

    Fixed Cost = 100,000
    Selling Price = 500
    Variable Cost = 300

Contribution margin per unit:

    500 - 300 = 200

Break-even units:

    100,000 / 200 = 500 units

The business must sell 500 units to cover its fixed costs under these assumptions.

---

# Break-Even Revenue

Break-even can also be calculated in terms of revenue.

The formula is:

    Break-even Revenue
    = Fixed Cost / Contribution Margin Ratio

Using:

    Fixed Cost = 100,000
    Contribution Margin Ratio = 40%

Break-even revenue is:

    100,000 / 0.40
    = 250,000

The script demonstrates both break-even units and break-even revenue.

---

# Important Break-Even Edge Cases

Break-even analysis has an important limitation.

If:

    Selling Price <= Variable Cost

then contribution margin is zero or negative.

In that situation, the standard break-even formula is not meaningful.

If contribution margin is zero:

    Fixed Cost / 0

is mathematically undefined.

If contribution margin is negative, increased sales do not cover fixed costs.

The script returns `None` for these situations rather than producing an invalid financial result.

---

# Gross Profit

Gross profit measures the amount remaining after deducting the cost of goods sold from revenue.

The formula is:

    Gross Profit = Revenue - Cost of Goods Sold

Cost of goods sold, often abbreviated as COGS, generally includes direct costs associated with producing or acquiring goods that were sold.

For example:

    Revenue = 1,000,000
    COGS = 600,000

Gross profit:

    1,000,000 - 600,000
    = 400,000

---

# Gross Margin

Gross margin expresses gross profit as a percentage of revenue.

The formula is:

    Gross Margin
    = Gross Profit / Revenue

Using:

    Gross Profit = 400,000
    Revenue = 1,000,000

Gross margin is:

    400,000 / 1,000,000
    = 40%

Gross margin measures profitability after deducting COGS but before deducting many other operating expenses.

The exact classification of costs between COGS and operating expenses can depend on accounting policy, industry, and business model.

---

# Operating Profit and Operating Margin

Operating profit measures profitability after deducting operating expenses from gross profit or contribution generated by the business.

A simplified relationship is:

    Operating Profit
    = Gross Profit
    - Operating Expenses

Operating margin is:

    Operating Margin
    = Operating Profit / Revenue

Operating expenses may include:

- Administrative salaries
- Marketing
- Rent
- Technology expenses
- Management costs
- General business expenses

The script demonstrates how operating profit differs from gross profit.

A company can have a high gross margin and a low operating margin if operating expenses are substantial.

---

# Net Profit and Net Margin

Net profit is the amount remaining after all relevant expenses have been deducted.

Depending on the financial model, these may include:

- Operating expenses
- Interest
- Taxes
- Other expenses

A simplified relationship is:

    Net Margin
    = Net Profit / Revenue

Net margin is often considered a more comprehensive profitability measure because it incorporates more categories of cost than gross margin.

---

# Comparison of Margin Types

Different margins answer different questions.

## Contribution Margin

Focus:

    Revenue minus variable costs

Useful for:

- Unit economics
- Break-even analysis
- Pricing
- Sales volume analysis
- Cost-volume-profit analysis

## Gross Margin

Focus:

    Revenue minus cost of goods sold

Useful for:

- Product economics
- Production efficiency
- Direct cost analysis

## Operating Margin

Focus:

    Operating profit relative to revenue

Useful for:

- Operating efficiency
- Cost structure analysis
- Core business profitability

## Net Margin

Focus:

    Final profit relative to revenue

Useful for:

- Overall profitability
- Financial performance
- Comparison across periods

These metrics should not be treated as interchangeable because they deduct different categories of costs.

# Unit Economics

Unit economics studies the revenue and cost associated with one unit of business activity.

The unit may represent:

- One product
- One order
- One transaction
- One customer
- One subscription
- One service engagement

The script uses the `UnitEconomics` class to calculate:

- Selling price
- Variable cost per unit
- Contribution margin
- Contribution margin ratio
- Whether unit economics are positive

Positive unit economics generally require:

    Revenue per Unit > Variable Cost per Unit

Positive unit economics do not guarantee total business profitability.

A company may have positive contribution margins but still generate a total loss if fixed costs exceed total contribution.

# Multi-Product Businesses

Most businesses do not operate with a single product.

A business may sell:

- Basic products
- Premium products
- Subscriptions
- Professional services
- Enterprise offerings

Each product can have different:

- Prices
- Variable costs
- Sales volumes
- Contribution margins

The script uses the `Product` and `Business` classes to model multi-product businesses.

The business calculates:

    Total Revenue
    = Sum of Product Revenues

    Total Variable Cost
    = Sum of Product Variable Costs

    Total Contribution Margin
    = Total Revenue - Total Variable Cost

    Operating Profit
    = Total Contribution Margin - Fixed Costs

This structure separates product-level economics from business-level fixed costs.

---

# Product Mix

Product mix describes the relative composition of products sold by a business.

Product mix matters because products can have very different margins.

For example:

    Product A:
    Revenue = 1,000,000
    Margin = 10%

    Product B:
    Revenue = 100,000
    Margin = 80%

A simple arithmetic average of margins would be misleading:

    (10% + 80%) / 2 = 45%

The correct combined margin depends on revenue weighting.

The correct formula is:

    Weighted Margin
    = Total Contribution Margin / Total Revenue

The script calculates weighted average contribution margin by aggregating total contribution and total revenue.

---

# Pricing

Price is a major driver of profitability.

A simplified unit relationship is:

    Contribution Margin
    = Price - Variable Cost

A higher price generally increases contribution margin if variable costs remain constant.

A lower price generally reduces contribution margin if variable costs remain constant.

Pricing decisions should therefore consider:

- Variable cost
- Fixed cost structure
- Demand
- Competitive conditions
- Customer value
- Required margins
- Expected sales volume

The script includes a formula for calculating the minimum price required to achieve a target contribution margin.

The relationship is:

    Target Margin
    = (Price - Variable Cost) / Price

Rearranging:

    Price
    = Variable Cost / (1 - Target Margin)

For example:

    Variable Cost = 600
    Target Margin = 40%

Required price:

    600 / (1 - 0.40)
    = 1,000

---

# Discounts and Margin Compression

Discounts reduce selling prices.

If variable costs remain unchanged, contribution margin can decline disproportionately.

For example:

Before discount:

    Price = 1,000
    Variable Cost = 600
    Contribution = 400
    Margin = 40%

After a 20% discount:

    New Price = 800
    Variable Cost = 600
    Contribution = 200
    Margin = 25%

Revenue per unit declined by 20%, but contribution per unit declined by 50%.

This is why discount decisions should not be evaluated only in terms of revenue growth.

A lower price may require significantly higher sales volume to generate the same total profit.

---

# Sales Volume Required for a Target Profit

The script calculates required sales volume using:

    Required Units
    = (Fixed Costs + Target Profit)
      / Contribution Margin per Unit

This formula can be used to compare pricing alternatives.

For example, if a discount reduces contribution margin per unit, the business must sell more units to generate the same target profit.

This creates a trade-off between:

- Lower prices and potentially higher demand
- Higher prices and higher contribution per sale

The optimal decision depends on how demand responds to price.

# Cost-Volume-Profit Analysis

Cost-volume-profit analysis studies the relationship between:

- Sales volume
- Selling price
- Variable costs
- Fixed costs
- Profit

The fundamental model is:

    Profit
    = Revenue
    - Variable Costs
    - Fixed Costs

For a single product:

    Profit
    = Units × Price
    - Units × Variable Cost per Unit
    - Fixed Cost

This can be rearranged as:

    Profit
    = Units × Contribution Margin per Unit
    - Fixed Cost

The script generates a table showing how revenue, total costs, and profit change at different sales volumes.

This type of analysis is useful for:

- Sales planning
- Production planning
- Pricing
- Forecasting
- Budgeting
- Capacity planning

# Operating Leverage

Operating leverage describes how sensitive operating profit is to changes in revenue.

A business with high fixed costs and relatively low variable costs generally has higher operating leverage.

Examples may include businesses with substantial:

- Infrastructure investments
- Salaried teams
- Software development costs
- Facilities
- Equipment

Once fixed costs are covered, additional revenue may contribute strongly to profit.

The opposite is also true.

If revenue declines, high fixed costs remain and profit can decline rapidly.

A simplified degree of operating leverage is:

    Degree of Operating Leverage
    = Contribution Margin / Operating Profit

The ratio becomes unstable when operating profit is close to zero.

The script explicitly handles zero operating profit because division by zero is mathematically undefined.

# Capacity Constraints

Demand does not automatically become revenue.

A business may have demand exceeding its ability to serve customers.

The script models this relationship:

    Actual Units Sold
    = Minimum of Market Demand and Production Capacity

For example:

    Market Demand = 1,000 units
    Production Capacity = 700 units

The business can sell only 700 units under the current capacity assumption.

Capacity constraints can affect:

- Revenue
- Contribution margin
- Profit
- Customer experience
- Growth potential

A business may need additional investment to increase capacity.

That investment can increase fixed costs, creating a trade-off between growth and cost structure.

---

# Step-Fixed Costs

Some costs are fixed only within specific capacity ranges.

These are called step-fixed costs.

For example:

One warehouse can handle:

    Up to 1,000 units

Warehouse cost:

    50,000

If demand reaches 1,001 units, the business may need another warehouse.

Cost then increases to:

    100,000

The cost structure therefore changes in steps rather than remaining perfectly flat.

The script calculates the number of facilities required based on total volume and capacity per facility.

---

# Mixed Costs

Mixed costs contain both fixed and variable components.

The formula is:

    Mixed Cost
    = Fixed Component
    + Variable Component × Units

An example is electricity:

- Fixed connection charge
- Variable consumption charge

Another example is a cloud service with:

- Base subscription fee
- Usage-based charges

Mixed costs should not be incorrectly classified as entirely fixed or entirely variable when both components exist.

---

# Margin Versus Markup

Margin and markup are frequently confused.

Assume:

    Cost = 600
    Selling Price = 1,000
    Profit = 400

Margin uses selling price as the denominator:

    Margin
    = Profit / Selling Price
    = 400 / 1,000
    = 40%

Markup uses cost as the denominator:

    Markup
    = Profit / Cost
    = 400 / 600
    = 66.67%

They are different measures.

A business that intends to achieve a 40% margin cannot simply add a 40% markup to cost.

The script demonstrates both calculations.

---

# Revenue Growth Versus Profit Growth

Revenue growth does not necessarily mean profit growth.

Consider two scenarios.

Scenario A:

    Revenue = 1,000,000
    Variable Costs = 500,000
    Fixed Costs = 300,000

Contribution margin:

    500,000

Profit:

    200,000

Scenario B:

    Revenue = 1,500,000
    Variable Costs = 1,250,000
    Fixed Costs = 300,000

Contribution margin:

    250,000

Profit:

    -50,000

Scenario B has higher revenue but lower profitability.

This demonstrates why revenue should be evaluated together with:

- Variable costs
- Contribution margins
- Fixed costs
- Product mix

# Customer-Level Economics

Customer economics extends unit economics to the customer level.

The script models:

- Monthly revenue
- Monthly variable cost
- Expected customer lifetime
- Customer acquisition cost

Lifetime revenue:

    Monthly Revenue × Expected Months

Lifetime variable cost:

    Monthly Variable Cost × Expected Months

Lifetime contribution:

    Lifetime Revenue - Lifetime Variable Cost

Customer profitability:

    Lifetime Contribution - Acquisition Cost

This framework is useful for subscription and recurring-revenue businesses.

It helps determine whether the economic value generated by a customer exceeds the cost of acquiring and serving that customer.

# Margin of Safety

Margin of safety measures how far actual sales exceed break-even sales.

The formula is:

    Margin of Safety
    = (Actual Sales - Break-even Sales)
      / Actual Sales

A larger margin of safety generally means the business has more room before falling below break-even.

For example:

    Actual Sales = 750 units
    Break-even Sales = 500 units

Margin of safety:

    (750 - 500) / 750
    = 33.33%

This indicates that sales could decline by approximately one-third before reaching the break-even level under unchanged assumptions.

- Zero sales combined with continuing fixed costs

Validation is important because incorrect assumptions can produce mathematically valid calculations that are economically meaningless.

The result is not necessarily useful simply because the calculation executed.

# Zero Revenue and Division by Zero

Margin calculations usually divide by revenue.

For example:

    Margin = Profit / Revenue

If revenue equals zero, the calculation would cause division by zero.

The script handles this case by returning zero for margin calculations where no revenue exists.

This is a modeling decision rather than a universal accounting rule.

The important principle is that software must explicitly define how edge cases are handled.

# Common Mistakes

## Treating Revenue as Profit

Revenue does not represent money remaining after costs.

A high-revenue company can have low or negative profit.

---

## Ignoring Variable Costs

Increasing sales volume does not automatically increase profit.

If variable costs rise substantially with each sale, additional revenue may produce little contribution.

---

## Treating All Costs as Fixed

Many costs increase with volume.

Ignoring this can produce unrealistic forecasts.

---

## Treating All Costs as Variable

Some costs remain stable across a range of sales levels.

Ignoring fixed costs can overstate profitability.

---

## Averaging Margin Percentages Incorrectly

Margin percentages should usually be weighted.

The correct business-level calculation is:

    Total Profit Measure / Total Revenue

rather than the simple arithmetic average of individual percentages.

---

## Assuming Discounts Require Only Proportional Volume Growth

A reduction in price can cause a much larger reduction in contribution margin.

The additional volume required to recover profit can therefore exceed the discount percentage.

---

## Ignoring Capacity

Forecast demand may exceed the organization's ability to produce or deliver.

Revenue forecasts should consider actual operational capacity.

---

## Using Break-Even Analysis with Negative Contribution Margins

If variable cost exceeds selling price, increased volume creates additional losses.

The standard break-even formula does not solve this problem.

    Profit

    Total Contribution Margin

    Contribution Margin Ratio

    Break-even Units

    Gross Margin

Cost behavior may also be nonlinear.

Shipping costs may increase in steps.

Infrastructure costs may remain fixed until a capacity threshold is reached and then increase significantly.

These factors require more detailed models when the decisions being made depend on them.

# Production Considerations

A production financial system should generally separate:

1. Source data
2. Assumptions
3. Calculation logic
4. Validation
5. Reporting

Important design principles include:

- Avoid hard-coded assumptions where frequent changes are expected.
- Validate all external inputs.
- Keep calculation functions deterministic.
- Test important formulas independently.
- Handle zero and boundary conditions explicitly.
- Preserve auditability of assumptions.
- Avoid mixing presentation logic with core financial calculations.
- Use consistent units and currencies.
- Document cost classifications.

The script demonstrates a simplified version of this separation through reusable functions and data classes.

# Core Conceptual Relationships

The central logic connecting the major concepts is:

    Revenue
        |
        v
    Subtract Variable Costs
        |
        v
    Contribution Margin
        |
        v
    Subtract Fixed Costs
        |
        v
    Operating Profit

Different margin measures provide different views of this process.

Contribution margin focuses on the relationship between sales and variable costs.

Gross margin focuses on revenue and direct cost of goods sold.

Operating margin focuses on operating profitability.

Net margin focuses on final profitability after additional expenses.

Understanding which costs are deducted at each stage is necessary for interpreting financial performance correctly.
