# How Businesses Create, Deliver and Capture Value

## 1. Introduction

A business exists because it solves a problem, satisfies a need, creates an experience, provides access to something valuable, or enables people and organizations to achieve a desired outcome.

The basic logic of business can be understood through three connected activities:

1. **Creating value**
2. **Delivering value**
3. **Capturing value**

These three activities explain much of what happens inside a business.

A company may create something that customers want but fail to deliver it effectively. Another company may deliver an excellent product but charge too little to remain profitable. A third company may have a profitable pricing model but offer something customers do not consider valuable.

A sustainable business needs a connection between all three.

The fundamental relationship can be expressed as:

```text
Business Value System

Create Value → Deliver Value → Capture Value
      ↑                              ↓
      └────── Resources, Feedback ───┘
```

Value creation concerns what the business offers and why it matters.

Value delivery concerns how the offering reaches customers and how the customer experiences it.

Value capture concerns how the business converts the value it creates into revenue, profit, cash flow, market position, or other economic returns.

A business model therefore cannot be understood only by looking at its product.

The product is only one component of a larger system.

---

# 2. What Is Value?

Value is the perceived benefit obtained by a customer or stakeholder relative to the costs, sacrifices, risks, and alternatives involved.

A simplified representation is:

```text
Perceived Value = Perceived Benefits - Perceived Costs
```

The equation is conceptual rather than strictly mathematical.

Benefits can include:

* Functional benefits
* Financial benefits
* Convenience
* Time savings
* Quality
* Reliability
* Status
* Safety
* Entertainment
* Emotional satisfaction
* Social recognition
* Reduced risk
* Increased productivity
* Access
* Belonging
* Personalization

Costs can include:

* Price
* Time
* Effort
* Learning requirements
* Switching costs
* Risk
* Complexity
* Uncertainty
* Opportunity cost
* Psychological effort

For example, a productivity application may cost ₹1,000 per year.

If a customer believes that the application saves 30 hours of work every year, reduces errors, and makes collaboration easier, the perceived value may be much greater than ₹1,000.

The customer is not purchasing software merely because software exists.

The customer is purchasing an expected outcome.

---

# 3. Value Is Not the Same as Price

Price and value are different concepts.

Price is what the customer pays.

Value is what the customer believes they receive.

Consider two products:

```text
Product A
Price = ₹500
Perceived Value = ₹700

Product B
Price = ₹5,000
Perceived Value = ₹12,000
```

Although Product B costs more, it can provide a stronger value proposition.

This is why customers do not always choose the cheapest alternative.

Customers often evaluate:

```text
Value
vs.
Price + Risk + Effort + Alternatives
```

A business can therefore compete through:

* Lower price
* Higher quality
* Better convenience
* Faster delivery
* Better experience
* Greater reliability
* Stronger brand
* Better service
* Specialized capabilities
* Unique functionality
* Lower risk

Value creation refers to the process through which a business transforms resources, knowledge, capabilities, technology, relationships, and activities into something that customers or other stakeholders consider valuable.

A simple representation is:

```text
Inputs → Activities → Offering → Customer Benefits
```

Inputs may include:

* Capital
* Labor
* Technology
* Data
* Raw materials
* Intellectual property
* Knowledge
* Relationships
* Infrastructure
* Brand
* Organizational capabilities

Activities transform these inputs.

Examples include:

* Research
* Product development
* Manufacturing
* Software development
* Marketing
* Distribution
* Customer support
* Financial management
* Logistics

The result is an offering that provides benefits to a customer.

# 6. Capabilities

Resources alone do not automatically create value.

A company may possess expensive technology but lack the capability to use it effectively.

A useful distinction is:

```text
Resources = What the organization has

Capabilities = What the organization can do with what it has
```

For example:

A company may own data.

That is a resource.

The ability to analyze the data, identify patterns, build useful products, and make better decisions is a capability.

Capabilities can include:

* Product development
* Supply-chain management
* Sales execution
* Customer acquisition
* Data analytics
* Innovation
* Manufacturing
* Quality management
* Risk management
* Organizational learning

---

# 7. Core Competencies

A core competency is a capability that is particularly important to the organization's ability to compete.

A strong competency generally:

* Creates meaningful customer value
* Is difficult for competitors to replicate
* Can support multiple products or markets

For example, a company may have exceptional logistics capabilities.

Those capabilities can support:

* Faster delivery
* Lower inventory costs
* Better customer experience
* Greater geographic coverage

The competency therefore influences both value creation and competitive advantage.

# 8. Value Proposition

The value proposition explains why a customer should choose an organization's offering.

A useful structure is:

```text
Customer Problem
        ↓
Desired Outcome
        ↓
Business Offering
        ↓
Specific Customer Benefit
        ↓
Reason to Choose This Offering
```

A strong value proposition answers questions such as:

* Who is the customer?
* What problem exists?
* What outcome does the customer want?
* What does the business provide?
* Why is the offering useful?
* Why is it better than alternatives?
* What sacrifice does the customer make?

For example:

```text
Customer:
Small business owner

Problem:
Managing invoices manually takes time

Offering:
Automated invoicing software

Benefit:
Invoices can be generated and tracked quickly

Value:
Less administrative work and faster payment collection
```

# 9. Customer Jobs

Customers do not necessarily want products for their own sake.

They often want a product to perform a job.

The "job" can be:

* Functional
* Emotional
* Social

A customer buying a drill may not fundamentally want a drill.

The customer may want a hole in a wall.

A customer using a ride-hailing application may not fundamentally want an application.

The customer wants transportation.

This distinction is important because businesses that focus too heavily on their existing product can miss changing customer needs.

The deeper question is:

> What outcome is the customer actually trying to achieve?

# 17. Value Capture

Value capture is the process through which a business converts the value created for customers into economic returns for itself.

The simplest form is:

```text
Customer Value
      ↓
Willingness to Pay
      ↓
Revenue
      ↓
Gross Profit
      ↓
Operating Profit
      ↓
Cash Flow
```

Value creation without value capture can result in an unsustainable business.

# 18. Revenue

Revenue represents the income generated from selling goods or services.

Basic revenue can be expressed as:

```text
Revenue = Price × Quantity Sold
```

For a subscription business:

```text
Revenue = Number of Customers × Subscription Price
```

For a marketplace:

```text
Revenue = Transaction Volume × Take Rate
```

For an advertising business:

```text
Revenue = Advertising Activity × Effective Advertising Price
```

Different business models therefore have different revenue equations.

---

## Subscription Revenue

## Usage-Based Revenue

# 20. Profit

Revenue alone does not mean a business is successful.

Profit depends on costs.

A basic representation is:

```text
Profit = Revenue - Costs
```

Costs can include:

* Raw materials
* Salaries
* Rent
* Technology
* Marketing
* Distribution
* Taxes
* Interest
* Customer support
* Research and development

A company can have high revenue and still lose money.

---

# 21. Gross Margin

Gross margin measures the portion of revenue remaining after direct costs associated with delivering the product or service.

```text
Gross Profit = Revenue - Cost of Goods Sold
```

```text
Gross Margin =
Gross Profit / Revenue
```

For example:

```text
Revenue = ₹10,00,000
COGS = ₹6,00,000

Gross Profit = ₹4,00,000

Gross Margin = 40%
```

Gross margin is important because it indicates how much economic value remains after direct delivery costs.

---

# 22. Operating Profit

Operating profit considers operating expenses such as:

* Sales
* Marketing
* Administration
* Research
* Product development

A simplified model is:

```text
Operating Profit
=
Gross Profit
-
Operating Expenses
```

This provides a clearer picture of whether the underlying business operations are economically sustainable.

---

# 23. Cash Flow

Profit and cash are not identical.

A company can report accounting profit while experiencing cash-flow problems.

Cash flow is influenced by:

* Customer payment timing
* Supplier payment timing
* Inventory
* Capital expenditure
* Debt repayments
* Working capital

A business ultimately needs sufficient liquidity to continue operating.

---

# 24. Willingness to Pay

Willingness to pay represents the maximum amount a customer is prepared to pay for an offering under particular conditions.

A customer may value the same product differently depending on:

* Income
* Urgency
* Alternatives
* Context
* Brand perception
* Switching costs
* Availability
* Risk

Therefore:

```text
Customer A's Willingness to Pay
≠
Customer B's Willingness to Pay
```

This difference creates opportunities for segmentation and pricing.

---

# 25. Consumer Surplus and Producer Surplus

Suppose a customer is willing to pay ₹10,000 for a product.

The company charges ₹7,000.

The customer's conceptual surplus is:

```text
₹10,000 - ₹7,000 = ₹3,000
```

This is consumer surplus.

The business also needs to consider its own costs and returns.

The relationship can be understood as:

```text
Total Economic Value
=
Consumer Surplus
+
Business Value Capture
```

The exact economic treatment depends on the model and assumptions, but the central idea is that total value can be divided between customers and producers.

# 26. Value Creation and Competitive Advantage

A company gains competitive advantage when it can create superior economic value relative to competitors.

Two broad approaches are:

```text
Higher Customer Value
or
Lower Cost
```

This can lead to:

* Differentiation
* Cost leadership
* Focused specialization

A differentiated company may charge a premium because customers perceive additional value.

A cost-efficient company may offer lower prices while maintaining acceptable margins.

---

# 27. Differentiation

Differentiation occurs when customers perceive meaningful differences between one company's offering and alternatives.

Sources include:

* Product features
* Quality
* Brand
* Design
* Customer service
* Technology
* Speed
* Convenience
* Reliability
* Ecosystem
* Customization

Differentiation only matters economically when customers recognize and value the difference.

A feature that customers do not care about may increase cost without increasing value.

---

# 28. Cost Advantage

A company can create value by operating at a lower cost than competitors.

Cost advantages can come from:

* Economies of scale
* Automation
* Efficient supply chains
* Better procurement
* Process optimization
* Technology
* Learning effects
* High asset utilization

For example:

```text
Competitor Cost per Unit = ₹80
Company Cost per Unit = ₹50
```

The company has more flexibility.

It can:

* Charge ₹80 and earn higher margins
* Charge ₹70 and compete aggressively
* Invest the savings in growth
* Spend more on customer acquisition

---

# 29. Economies of Scale

Economies of scale occur when average cost decreases as output increases.

```text
Average Cost = Total Cost / Output
```

Suppose:

```text
10 units → ₹1,000 total cost → ₹100/unit

100 units → ₹5,000 total cost → ₹50/unit
```

The cost per unit falls as production expands.

This can create barriers to entry because new competitors may initially operate at higher unit costs.

---

# 30. Economies of Scope

Economies of scope occur when producing multiple products together is cheaper or more valuable than producing them independently.

For example, a company may use the same:

* Brand
* Distribution network
* Technology platform
* Sales force
* Customer database

across multiple products.

This allows one capability to support several revenue streams.

---

# 31. Network Effects

A network effect occurs when the value of a product or service changes as the number of participating users changes.

For example:

```text
More Users
   ↓
More Connections / Content / Liquidity
   ↓
Higher Customer Value
   ↓
More Users
```

Examples include:

* Social networks
* Marketplaces
* Communication platforms
* Payment networks

Network effects can create powerful competitive advantages.

They can also create winner-take-most market structures in certain circumstances.

---

# 32. Direct and Indirect Network Effects

A direct network effect occurs when users benefit directly from having more users.

Example:

```text
Messaging platform
More users → More people available to communicate with
```

An indirect network effect occurs when growth on one side of a platform increases value for another side.

For example:

```text
More Buyers
     ↓
More attractive marketplace
     ↓
More Sellers
     ↓
More Product Selection
     ↓
More Buyers
```

---

Platform businesses connect different groups.

Examples of platform participants may include:

* Buyers and sellers

* Advertisers and audiences

The platform creates value by facilitating interactions.

Its core activities can include:

* Matching
* Search

* Trust
* Payments
* Reputation

Platform businesses often need to solve the chicken-and-egg problem.

A marketplace with no sellers has little value to buyers.

# 34. Transaction Costs

Businesses can create value by reducing transaction costs.

Transaction costs can include:

* Searching

For example:

```text

Platform:
Search → Match → Verify → Pay → Coordinate
```

Reducing friction can itself be a powerful value proposition.

Businesses have different cost structures.

Costs can be:

* Fixed
* Variable
* Semi-variable
* Direct
* Indirect

## Fixed Costs

Costs that do not change directly with short-term output.

Examples:

## Variable Costs

Costs that change with production or sales.

Examples:

* Raw materials
* Payment processing

---

# 38. Contribution Margin

Contribution margin measures how much revenue remains after variable costs.

```text
Contribution Margin
=
Revenue - Variable Costs
```

Contribution margin per unit:

```text
Selling Price - Variable Cost per Unit
```

Suppose:

```text
Selling price = ₹1,000
Variable cost = ₹400

Contribution = ₹600
```

That ₹600 contributes toward fixed costs and profit.

---

# 39. Break-Even Analysis

Break-even occurs when total revenue equals total costs.

```text
Profit = 0
```

For a simple business:

```text
Break-even Units
=
Fixed Costs /
Contribution Margin per Unit
```

Suppose:

```text
Fixed Costs = ₹6,00,000
Selling Price = ₹1,000
Variable Cost = ₹400

Contribution = ₹600
```

Then:

```text
Break-even Units
=
₹6,00,000 / ₹600
=
1,000 units
```

The business must sell approximately 1,000 units to cover its fixed costs under these assumptions.

---

# 40. Customer Acquisition Cost

Customer acquisition cost, commonly called CAC, estimates the cost required to acquire a customer.

A simplified formula is:

```text
CAC =
Sales and Marketing Costs /
Number of New Customers
```

Suppose:

```text
Marketing + Sales = ₹10,00,000
New Customers = 1,000
```

Then:

```text
CAC = ₹1,000
```

CAC must be evaluated against the economic value generated by customers.

---

# 41. Customer Lifetime Value

Customer lifetime value, commonly called LTV or CLV, estimates the economic value generated by a customer over the relationship.

A simplified subscription model can be represented as:

```text
LTV ≈
Average Revenue per Customer × Gross Margin × Customer Lifetime
```

For example:

```text
Monthly Revenue = ₹1,000
Gross Margin = 80%
Average Lifetime = 20 months

LTV ≈
₹1,000 × 0.80 × 20
=
₹16,000
```

The exact calculation depends on churn, discount rates, expansion revenue, retention, and other factors.

---

# 42. CAC and LTV

A business should understand the relationship between customer acquisition cost and customer lifetime value.

Conceptually:

```text
LTV > CAC
```

is necessary for customer acquisition to generate positive economic value, although the acceptable relationship depends on business structure and timing.

A business with:

```text
CAC = ₹1,000
LTV = ₹500
```

has an obvious economic problem if those figures are reliable.

A company can grow rapidly while destroying value if it acquires customers at a cost greater than their economic contribution.

---

# 43. Churn

Churn measures customer loss.

For a subscription business:

```text
Customer Churn Rate
=
Customers Lost During Period /
Customers at Beginning of Period
```

High churn can weaken customer lifetime value.

A business therefore creates value not only by acquiring customers but also by retaining them.

Retention can depend on:

* Product quality
* Customer satisfaction
* Switching costs
* Service
* Pricing
* Habit
* Network effects
* Product integration

---

# 44. Recurring Revenue

Recurring revenue is revenue expected to repeat periodically.

Examples:

* Software subscriptions
* Memberships
* Maintenance contracts
* Insurance
* Telecommunications

Recurring revenue can make revenue more predictable.

Common metrics include:

* Monthly recurring revenue
* Annual recurring revenue
* Churn
* Retention
* Expansion revenue

---

# 45. Pricing

Pricing is one of the most important mechanisms for value capture.

The price must balance:

```text
Customer Willingness to Pay
        ↓
Competitive Alternatives
        ↓
Business Costs
        ↓
Strategic Objectives
```

Pricing should not be determined solely by cost.

A cost-plus approach is:

```text
Price = Cost + Markup
```

A value-based approach begins with:

```text
Customer Value
↓
Willingness to Pay
↓
Appropriate Price
```

Competitive pricing considers the prices and positions of alternatives.

---

# 46. Price Discrimination

Price discrimination involves charging different customers different prices for substantially similar offerings based on differences in willingness to pay or other relevant conditions.

Examples can include:

* Student pricing
* Enterprise pricing
* Geographic pricing
* Volume discounts
* Time-based pricing

The purpose is often to capture more of the available economic value.

---

# 47. Dynamic Pricing

Dynamic pricing changes prices according to changing conditions.

Variables can include:

* Demand
* Supply
* Time
* Capacity
* Customer behavior
* Inventory
* Market conditions

A simplified relationship is:

```text
High Demand + Limited Supply
        ↓
Higher Potential Price
```

Dynamic pricing is common in industries with variable capacity or rapidly changing demand.

---

# 48. Bundling

Bundling involves selling multiple products or services together.

Examples include:

```text
Product A
+
Product B
+
Product C

Bundle
```

Bundling can:

* Increase average transaction value
* Simplify purchasing
* Increase product adoption
* Improve cross-selling
* Reduce price comparison
* Capture different willingness-to-pay patterns

---

# 49. Cross-Selling and Upselling

Cross-selling encourages customers to purchase related products.

Example:

```text
Laptop
+
Accessories
+
Software
```

Upselling encourages customers to purchase a higher-value version.

Example:

```text
Basic Plan
→
Professional Plan
→
Enterprise Plan
```

Both can increase customer value capture.

---

Businesses rarely operate alone.

An ecosystem can contain:

* Customers
* Suppliers
* Technology providers
* Distributors
* Regulators
* Partners
* Complementors
* Competitors

A company may create more value by coordinating an ecosystem rather than producing everything internally.

For example:

```text
Core Platform
      ↓
Developers
      ↓
Applications
      ↓
Customers
```

The ecosystem can increase the usefulness of the core offering.

---

# 51. Complementors

A complementor provides a product or service that increases the value of another product.

Examples:

```text
Operating System + Applications

Gaming Console + Games

Smartphone + Applications

Payment Network + Merchant Acceptance
```

The success of one participant can increase the value of another participant.

---

# 52. Vertical Integration

Vertical integration occurs when a company expands into activities earlier or later in its value chain.

For example:

```text
Raw Materials
      ↓
Manufacturing
      ↓
Distribution
      ↓
Retail
```

A company may control multiple stages.

Potential advantages include:

* Greater control
* Lower transaction costs
* Supply security
* Quality control
* Better coordination

Potential disadvantages include:

* Higher capital requirements
* Reduced flexibility
* Management complexity
* Greater operational risk

---

# 53. Outsourcing

A business may choose to obtain activities from external providers.

Reasons include:

* Lower cost
* Specialized expertise
* Flexibility
* Faster scaling
* Focus on core capabilities

The decision can be analyzed by asking:


The answer depends on:

* Cost
* Strategic importance
* Capability
* Control

# 72. Payback Period

CAC payback period estimates how long it takes to recover customer acquisition cost through contribution generated by the customer.

A simplified representation is:

```text
CAC Payback Period
=
CAC /
Monthly Contribution per Customer
```

Suppose:

```text
CAC = ₹3,000
Monthly Contribution = ₹500
```

Then:

```text
Payback Period = 6 months
```

The shorter the payback period, the faster the business recovers the acquisition investment, assuming the underlying assumptions are reliable.

# 102. The Central Business Logic

The entire concept of how businesses create, deliver, and capture value can be understood through three questions:

```text
1. CREATE
What valuable outcome can we provide?

2. DELIVER
How can we reliably get that value to the customer?

3. CAPTURE
How can we earn enough economic return from providing it?
```

The three questions are interdependent.

A company cannot sustainably capture value without creating something customers value.

It cannot create practical customer value without delivering the offering effectively.

It cannot remain economically sustainable without capturing enough value to cover its resources, activities, risks, and investments.

The business therefore operates as an interconnected economic system rather than as a collection of independent departments.
